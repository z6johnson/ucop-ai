## item-1 — OpenAI releases GPT-6 Astra, its first model rated 'Critical' for cybersecurity capability under its own Preparedness Framework

### What happened
On September 3, 2026, OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra, describing it as its most capable broadly deployed model to date. OpenAI's own safety overview designates Astra as the first model to reach the 'Critical' cybersecurity capability threshold under its Preparedness Framework, meaning the company assessed it as capable of providing meaningful uplift to sophisticated cyberattackers. OpenAI states it implemented stronger safeguards before release, including enhanced monitoring and access controls.

### Why it matters to UC
UC's systemwide security posture for AI tools is anchored to IS-3 and prohibits sharing personal information with AI tools lacking a fully executed data agreement — but the baseline records no systemwide process for evaluating whether a newly released frontier model's capability jump triggers a reassessment of existing tool approvals or data agreements. UCLA, UCI (ZotGPT), UCSD (TritonGPT alongside licensed ChatGPT), and UCSF all have active OpenAI deployments approved under prior-generation model assumptions. A vendor self-declared 'Critical' cybersecurity rating on a model now available through those contracts is a material change that UC's security framework has not yet addressed at the system level.

### For the committee
Should UCOP establish a trigger-based review process — tied to vendor-declared capability thresholds — that requires campuses with active OpenAI agreements to re-verify their data-classification approvals before migrating users to GPT-6 Astra?

## item-5 — Committee co-chair Khosla publicly describes UC AI Steering Committee's coordination challenge; no systemwide AI platform yet exists

### What happened
In a podcast interview published August 19–20, 2026 and surfaced this week, committee co-chair Chancellor Pradeep Khosla described his role leading the UC AI Steering Committee and articulated the coordination challenges of governing AI across ten campuses, six health systems, and three national labs. Khosla framed UCSD's decision to build and operate TritonGPT in-house — rather than relying on a vendor contract — as a model worth examining at the system level.

### Why it matters to UC
Khosla's public framing surfaces a live governance tension the baseline documents: there is no systemwide enterprise AI platform, and UC Davis's AI Council report explicitly notes that 'the lack of an effective and financially accessible UC-wide genAI tool necessitates continued local development.' Campuses have independently built or licensed at least five distinct platforms (TritonGPT, ZotGPT, River/Gemini at Berkeley, Gemini Enterprise at UCR, AggieAI at Davis), each with different data classification postures, vendor dependencies, and cost structures. The committee co-chair's public characterization of this as a coordination challenge — not yet resolved — signals that the question of whether UC should pursue a shared platform is moving from background assumption to active agenda item.

Given the co-chair's public framing and Davis's documented call for systemwide pooled investment, does the committee want to place a shared-platform feasibility assessment on its formal agenda before campuses make additional multi-year vendor commitments?
